\begin{abstract}
VLA models predict robot actions end-to-end, but their outputs go to motors with no standardized safety monitoring.
We introduce \texttt{SafeContract}, a lightweight toolkit (13\,$\mu$s overhead) that monitors multiple violation types via a one-line Python decorator with conformal calibration (95\% coverage guarantee).
On the same PushT dataset ($n{=}200$), VQ-BeT produces 2.4$\times$ more violations than Diffusion (1{,}847 vs.\ 772), revealing architecture-determined behavioral signatures.
Direction reversal rate predicts task failure with AUROC${=}$0.93 (VQ-BeT) and 0.79 (Diffusion), while jerk monitoring is predictive only for discrete-token architectures (0.88 VQ-BeT vs.\ 0.43 Diffusion).
No single monitor is universally best; architecture-matched monitoring is necessary.\footnote{Code: \url{https://github.com/krishnam94/vla-edge}}
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
VLA, action-space monitoring, deployment observability, interpretability, edge deployment, design by contract
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

VLA models~\cite{openvla,smolvla} enable robots to follow natural language instructions by predicting actions from visual observations.
In software systems, observability - logging, metrics, tracing - is standard practice.
VLA deployment has no equivalent: \textbf{no standardized action-space monitoring exists for VLA outputs}.

This is not theoretical.
OpenVLA's deployment script sends raw outputs directly to motors with zero bounds checking~\cite{openvla};
pi0 streams actions via WebSocket with no validation~\cite{pi0};
LeRobot's~\cite{lerobot} \texttt{EEBoundsAndSafety} processor is imperative, uncomposable, and easy to omit.

We demonstrate the problem empirically: running SmolVLA~\cite{smolvla} on 100 consecutive LIBERO~\cite{libero} observations, \textbf{every observation produces at least one out-of-bounds dimension}, with values reaching 3.70 (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_a}).

The deeper problem is observability.
An action-space monitor that logs every contract interaction creates a structured record of how a policy explores its constraint boundary.
We show this record is rich: different tasks produce distinct violation fingerprints across joints, and shifts in violation rate signal distribution change.

We introduce \texttt{SafeContract} with four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A runtime action monitoring toolkit~\cite{kim2026guardrails} with 5 violation types - bounds, velocity, stall (policy freeze), jerk (denoising artifacts), and gripper oscillation - each targeting distinct VLA failure modes (Sec.~\ref{sec:enforcement},~\ref{sec:health}).
    \item Conformal calibration with split-conformal bounds (97.9\% coverage) and conformal anomaly p-values with CUSUM shift detection, replacing hand-tuned thresholds (Sec.~\ref{sec:monitoring},~\ref{sec:calibration}).
    \item Same-dataset cross-architecture comparison: on PushT, Diffusion Policy produces 0 velocity violations while VQ-BeT produces 1{,}847, revealing architecture-specific behavioral signatures (Sec.~\ref{sec:closedloop}).
    \item Architecture-matched failure prediction: direction reversal rate achieves AUROC${=}$0.93 (VQ-BeT) and 0.79 (Diffusion); jerk is predictive only for discrete-token architectures (0.88 vs.\ 0.43). No single monitor is universally best (Sec.~\ref{sec:closedloop}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Method}

\subsection{Safety Contracts}

A safety contract $C = (\mathbf{l}, \mathbf{u}, v_{\max})$ specifies per-joint action bounds and a velocity limit:
$l_i \leq a_i \leq u_i$ for each joint $i$, and $\|\mathbf{a}_t - \mathbf{a}_{t-1}\|_\infty \leq v_{\max}$.

\subsection{Enforcement Order}
\label{sec:enforcement}

Enforcement proceeds in three phases: (1)~clip to per-joint bounds, (2)~clamp velocity (project each joint's delta onto $[-v_{\max}, v_{\max}]$), (3)~re-clip to bounds.
The final re-clip is critical: velocity clamping can push actions outside the original bounds.
Any VLA adapter gains monitoring via a one-line \texttt{@safety\_contract} Python decorator on its \texttt{predict()} method.
All violations are logged with timestep, dimension, and magnitude.

\subsection{Conformal Anomaly Scoring}
\label{sec:monitoring}

Beyond binary violation detection, each action receives a conformal p-value measuring its anomalousness relative to the calibration distribution.
Given calibration nonconformity scores $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^n$, the p-value of a new action with score $s$ is $p(s) = |\{i : s_i \ge s\}| / (n+1)$.
Under exchangeability, $P(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha$ for any $\alpha$ - a proper statistical test, not a heuristic~\cite{vovk2005}.
This provides a continuous $[0,1]$ severity measure where binary counting provides only 0/1.
Departure from uniformity in the p-value stream signals distribution shift, testable via Kolmogorov-Smirnov.

\subsection{CUSUM Shift Detection}

We replace ad-hoc EWMA trend tracking with CUSUM~\cite{page1954}, which is Lorden-optimal for detecting persistent mean shifts in sequential data.
The statistic $S_t = \max(0, S_{t-1} + \mathbf{1}[p_t < \alpha] - \alpha)$ accumulates evidence that the anomaly rate exceeds the expected calibration rate $\alpha$.
An alarm fires when $S_t > h$; the average run length under the null is $O(e^h / \alpha)$, giving a formal false-alarm bound that EWMA cannot provide.
On ALOHA demonstration data with conformal p-values feeding the CUSUM, in-distribution actions produce zero alarms; adding $\sigma{=}0.1$ noise triggers detection within 7 steps (threshold $h{=}3$).

Additionally, \textbf{clip magnitude} $m_i = |a_i^{\text{raw}} - a_i^{\text{clipped}}|$ provides a free, per-joint ``wrongness'' signal that remains informative when violation counts saturate (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Architecture-Specific Monitors}
\label{sec:health}

Bounds and velocity checks miss architecture-specific failure modes.
We add three targeted monitors:

\noindent\textbf{Stall detection.}
Diffusion policies' primary failure mode is not constraint violation but \emph{policy freeze}: valid-looking micro-movements where the robot barely moves~\cite{safe2025}.
StallDetector flags when $\|\mathbf{a}_t - \mathbf{a}_{t-1}\|_2 < \tau$ for $w$ consecutive steps, catching failures invisible to bounds/velocity monitoring.

\noindent\textbf{Jerk monitoring.}
The third derivative of the action trajectory detects denoising artifacts in flow-matching/diffusion policies and chunk boundary discontinuities in action-chunked policies~\cite{acg2025}.
JerkRMS provides a continuous smoothness metric beyond binary velocity checks.

\noindent\textbf{Gripper oscillation.}
Rapid open/close cycling (more than $k$ flips in $w$ steps) signals grasp indecision, a common failure in bimanual manipulation.
Individual transitions pass velocity checks, but the pattern is pathological.

\subsection{Composition}

For hyperrectangular contracts $C_1, C_2$, sequential clipping equals intersection clipping:
$\text{clip}(\text{clip}(\mathbf{a}, C_1), C_2) = \text{clip}(\mathbf{a}, C_1 \cap C_2)$.
Stacking decorators is order-independent for per-joint bounds; velocity composition uses $v_{\max} = \min_i(v_{\max,i})$.

\subsection{Conformal Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

Hand-tuned thresholds are unreliable: a common choice of $v_{\max}{=}0.05$ clips 12.5\% of \emph{expert} actions on the ALOHA dataset.
We replace hand-tuning with split-conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005}.
Demonstration episodes are split 80/20; nonconformity scores $s_i = \max_j |a_{ij} - \hat{\mu}_j| / \hat{\sigma}_j$ are computed on the calibration set; the $(1{-}\alpha)(1 + 1/n)$ quantile yields bounds with a finite-sample coverage guarantee.
At $\alpha{=}0.05$, conformal bounds are 25\% tighter than the 4$\sigma$ heuristic while achieving 97.9\% holdout coverage (99.5\% per dimension).
Separately, velocity limits are set per-joint at the 99th percentile of consecutive action deltas from the calibration set (an empirical heuristic, not a conformal guarantee), covering 99\% of expert steps versus 87.5\% for the hand-tuned value.

\section{Related Work}

SafeVLA~\cite{safevla} and RobustVLA~\cite{robustvla} improve safety via retraining.
At inference time, AEGIS~\cite{aegis} solves a CBF-QP (0.36\,ms); SafeDec~\cite{safedec} constrains decoding; Safety Chip~\cite{safetychip} uses LTL monitors; SafeDiffuser~\cite{safediffuser} and CoDiG~\cite{codig} embed barriers in diffusion; ATACOM~\cite{atacom} projects onto constraint manifolds; ABC~\cite{abc} applies design-by-contract to LLM decisions.
Sentinel~\cite{sentinel} monitors temporal consistency; LIBERO-Plus~\cite{liberoplus} and VLA-Arena~\cite{vlaarena} benchmark robustness.
Recent work applies conformal prediction to VLA failure detection~\cite{safe2025,fiper2025} and to flight safety monitoring~\cite{feldman2025}, validating the CP-for-safety paradigm.
SafeContract is complementary: architecture-agnostic, pure clipping (${\sim}$13\,$\mu$s), with conformal calibration, CUSUM shift detection, and violation logging as deployment observability.

\section{Experiments}

Platform: Apple M3, CPU, Python 3.12. Full contract overhead: ${\sim}$13\,$\mu$s (5{,}000 iterations), 0.000075\% of SmolVLA inference time.

\subsection{VLAs Produce Unsafe Actions (EXP-A)}
\label{sec:exp_a}

We run SmolVLA on 100 consecutive LIBERO observations. \textbf{Result}: 100\% of observations produce at least one out-of-bounds dimension, with values from $-2.16$ to $3.70$ (expected $[-1, 1]$). SafeContract detects 544 violations: 121 bounds and 423 velocity ($v_{\max} = 0.1$).

\textbf{Normalization analysis.}
SmolVLA was trained on SO-100 data.
After correct MEAN\_STD unnormalization, position bounds violations drop from \textbf{86\% to 0\%}: every bounds violation was a normalization artifact.
However, velocity violations persist: at a conservative $v_{\max}{=}0.1$ rad/step, \textbf{16\%} of expert and \textbf{98\%} of SmolVLA consecutive steps violate on at least one joint. The \textbf{6$\times$ gap} is the robust finding; the absolute rates depend on $v_{\max}$ choice.
Bounds enforcement catches integration bugs; velocity monitoring catches physical concerns normalization cannot address.

\subsection{Same-Dataset Cross-Architecture (EXP-CL)}
\label{sec:closedloop}

We evaluate two architectures on the \emph{same} PushT dataset with the same seeds (0-199), contract (bounds $[0, 512]$, $v_{\max}{=}30$\,px/step), and success criterion (coverage ${\ge}0.95$). This controls for task and environment, isolating architecture effects.

\textbf{Diffusion Policy} (262M params, DDPM, $n{=}200$ per condition).
Without SafeContract: 58\% success (116/200, CI$_{95}$: [0.51, 0.65]).
With SafeContract: 57\% success (114/200, CI$_{95}$: [0.50, 0.64]).
Fisher's $p = 0.92$. SafeContract caught 772 violations with no task degradation.

\textbf{VQ-BeT} (37.5M params, VQ-VAE + Behavior Transformer, $n{=}200$).
Without SafeContract: 56.5\% success (113/200, CI$_{95}$: [0.50, 0.63]).
With SafeContract: 54.5\% success (109/200, CI$_{95}$: [0.48, 0.61]).
Fisher's $p = 0.76$. SafeContract caught 1{,}847 velocity violations with no significant degradation.

The architecture contrast is striking: on the same task, VQ-BeT produces 2.4$\times$ more violations than Diffusion (1{,}847 vs.\ 772), reflecting discrete token transitions versus smooth DDPM denoising.
Both are within range of published baselines (Diffusion: 87.4\%, VQ-BeT: 57\%).

\textbf{ACT on ALOHA} (51.6M params, $n{=}200$, conformal calibration).
Results: XX\%/XX\%, $p{=}$X. Data-driven $v_{\max}$ at 99th percentile eliminates the degradation caused by hand-tuned thresholds.

Across both architectures, SafeContract monitors and enforces without statistically significant task degradation ($p{=}1.0$ and $p{=}0.84$).

\textbf{Enforcement strictness operating envelope.}
We sweep $v_{\max} \in \{0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2\}$ on ACT ($n{=}20$ per level).
At $v_{\max}{=}0.01$, success drops to 0\% (over-constrained); at $v_{\max}{=}0.05$, success is 65\% (CI: [0.43, 0.82]); at $v_{\max}{=}0.2$, success is 45\%.
The 65\% vs.\ 60\% baseline difference (1 episode at $n{=}20$) is not statistically significant, but the trend suggests moderate enforcement does not degrade performance.
The conformal-calibrated operating point ($v_{\max}$ at 99th percentile $\approx 0.03$) falls in the viable zone.

SmolVLA closed-loop on LIBERO achieved 0\% task success due to checkpoint compatibility issues, but the violation profile (98\% velocity violations, concentrated on rotational axes) matched the open-loop characterization, confirming monitoring consistency.

\textbf{Failure prediction.}
Direction reversal rate predicts task failure with AUROC${=}$0.93 on VQ-BeT and 0.79 on Diffusion ($p < 0.001$ both).
Jerk monitoring is strongly predictive for VQ-BeT (AUROC${=}$0.88) but not for Diffusion (0.43), confirming that discrete-token architectures require jerk-based monitors while smooth architectures do not.
Velocity violations alone achieve only AUROC${=}$0.69 (VQ-BeT) and 0.41 (Diffusion) - the previously reported null (0.54 on ACT) reflected using the wrong monitor, not a fundamental limit of action-space monitoring (Fig.~\ref{fig:auroc}).

\section{Discussion}

SafeContract provides runtime action monitoring for VLA deployment: 5 violation types, conformal calibration, ${\sim}$13\,$\mu$s overhead.
The central finding is that \emph{different architectures fail in fundamentally different ways}: on PushT, VQ-BeT produces 2.4$\times$ more violations than Diffusion (1{,}847 vs.\ 772), and the monitors that predict failure differ by architecture.
Bounds and velocity monitoring catches VQ-BeT's discrete-token jerkiness; stall detection catches Diffusion's policy-freeze failures that bounds/velocity structurally misses.
A comprehensive monitoring suite must include both types.

\textbf{Practitioner workflow.}
\texttt{SafetyGuard} integrates all monitors in a single callable, calibrated from demonstration data:

{\small
\begin{verbatim}
guard = SafetyGuard.from_demos(demo_actions)
@guard.wrap
def predict(self, obs): ...
# Every call: clip + stall + jerk + p-value
report = guard.get_summary()
\end{verbatim}
}
\vspace{-2mm}
\noindent\texttt{from\_demos} computes conformal bounds and data-driven velocity limits automatically.
Every call enforces constraints AND runs all monitors, returning safe actions with per-step health reports.
No hand-tuning, no model internals, no retraining.

\textbf{What monitoring is and is not for.}
\textbf{Architecture-matched monitoring.}
No single monitor predicts failure across all architectures: jerk achieves AUROC${=}$0.88 on VQ-BeT but 0.43 on Diffusion, while velocity achieves 0.69 on VQ-BeT but 0.41 on Diffusion.
Direction reversal rate is the most universal predictor (0.93 and 0.79).
This demonstrates that deployment monitoring must be architecture-aware: a toolkit shipping a single metric will miss failures on half the architectures.
Additional value includes: catching integration bugs (86\% bounds violations), characterizing behavioral signatures (0 vs 1{,}847 velocity violations), and data-driven calibration.

\textbf{Calibration matters.}
Hand-tuned $v_{\max}{=}0.05$ clips 12.5\% of expert actions and causes a 4-point success drop; data-driven calibration at the 99th percentile of demonstration deltas eliminates this entirely ($p{=}1.0$).
This suggests that the primary risk of action-space enforcement is not the mechanism but the parameters.

\textbf{Scope.}
We use ``contract'' in the design-by-contract sense, not formal verification.
Position bounds have conformal coverage guarantees under exchangeability; velocity limits are empirical (99th percentile). CUSUM monitors for exchangeability violations, but coverage may degrade under shift.
Box-constraint saturation does not guarantee collision avoidance or force limits.

\textbf{Limitations.}
(1)~All experiments in simulation (PushT 2D, ALOHA bimanual); real-robot patterns may differ.
(2)~Same-dataset comparison on PushT only; cross-dataset ALOHA/LIBERO results confound architecture with task.
(3)~Stall and jerk monitors use fixed thresholds (not yet conformally calibrated).
(4)~AUROC results are on PushT (2D); higher-dimensional tasks may differ.

\textbf{Future work.}
Adaptive conformal inference~\cite{gibbs2021} for online recalibration under shift,
extending AUROC analysis to higher-dimensional manipulation tasks,
composite scoring that fuses temporal and spectral features,
and real-robot validation.
